## 2. Narrative on the top five

Ranked by a combination of likelihood and how soon each becomes actionable, not strictly by the impact column alone — R2 and R10 are lower-likelihood-going-forward but are included because one has already occurred and the other, while rare, is close to existential if mishandled.

### R1 — vendor sources break faster than they can be repaired

**What materialising looks like:** a source that was passing conditional checks and extracting cleanly starts returning selector misses — not a fetch failure, but `Extract` producing zero candidates (or malformed ones) from a `200 OK` response, because the vendor redesigned their page, moved their changelog, or changed their markup framework. In the MVP this shows up as a specific fixture that used to pass now failing after a maintainer manually re-checks it against the live site (never automatically, per collector-sdk.md §9's rule that fixtures are never live-site-dependent), or as a `review_items` backlog growing because low-confidence candidates keep landing there with no throughput to clear them. At small scale (four pilot vendors) this is a nuisance; at the scale the roadmap eventually targets (hundreds to thousands of sources), it is the central operational risk the entire AI-escalation design (blueprint §3.11, §15, mvp-roadmap.md Phase 6) exists to eventually address — and until Phase 6 ships, there is no automated repair path at all, only manual maintainer attention.

**First action to take:** when a source's extraction confidence or candidate count visibly drops for reasons other than a genuine absence of new releases, treat it exactly like a bug report — file it, do not silently patch the config and move on without recording why, because the "why" (what changed on the vendor's site, and when) is exactly the training signal a future Repair agent needs and exactly the kind of institutional knowledge R11 is worried about losing.

### R2 — a vendor sends a legal demand

**What materialising looks like:** any contact — an email to the security or vendor-relations address, a GitHub issue, a formal letter — that references legal terms, cites specific vendor content being reproduced without permission, or asserts FirmScout is violating terms of service or exceeding authorised access. It could be about evidence excerpt length, about a source that should have been marked `disallowed` and was not, or about something the compliance review process (DATA_SOURCES.md) simply had not yet reached for a newly registered vendor.

**First action to take:** treat it with the same urgency and care as a security report (SECURITY.md's existing channel is explicitly named as the model to follow in DATA_SOURCES.md's removal-request process) — acknowledge quickly, do not argue the legal merits informally over email, disable the specific source(s) in question immediately as a good-faith gesture regardless of whether FirmScout believes it is in the right, and get the actual legal questions (several of which are pre-registered in licensing.md §9, e.g. question 3 on robots.txt collection and question 6 on removal requests) in front of qualified counsel before responding substantively. Acting first and arguing later is the wrong order; disabling first and then evaluating the legal merits calmly is the right one.

### R5 — the community never contributes

**What materialising looks like:** this is a slow-motion risk, not a single event — the signal is an absence, measured over quarters, not an incident. Concretely: Phase 2 ships a review-queue UI intended partly to make external contribution more approachable, and two quarters later the number of externally authored (non-founder) collector configs merged is still zero or near-zero, despite the project being public and the "config-driven collector needs no Go knowledge" design point (collector-sdk.md §10) being real and not just aspirational.

**First action to take:** before concluding the barrier is technical (harder-than-expected YAML authoring) and building more tooling to lower it, check whether the barrier is actually *discoverability* or *trust* instead — does anyone outside the founder know FirmScout exists and accepts collector contributions at all, and does the project's own documentation (starting with `docs/collectors/developing-collectors.md`, which already exists) actually reach someone who is not already deep in the codebase. Assumption P5 is marked unvalidated for a reason; the first action is diagnosing *which* assumption failed, not reflexively building more contributor tooling on a guess.

### R6 — the founder is a single point of failure

**What materialising looks like:** there is no single incident that signals this risk cleanly — it is realized the moment something urgent needs a decision (a legal contact per R2, a source that broke per R1, a data-correctness dispute per R7) and the founder is unavailable, and nobody else has enough context to act. The absence of an early-warning *metric* for this risk is itself notable and is stated plainly in the table above rather than papered over with a fabricated signal.

**First action to take:** the mitigation is preventive, not reactive — it is the discipline this entire document set already embodies: write the reasoning down, not just the decision (every ADR's "Alternatives considered" section, every DATA_SOURCES.md entry's "why," this risk register's own narrative sections) specifically so that a second person reading only the repository, with no access to the founder, could make a reasonable next decision. The first concrete action, once the project has any contributors or collaborators at all, is identifying at least one other person who can be reached for exactly the R2-shaped situation (a legal or vendor-relations contact needing a same-day response) — a single point of failure for *routine* operational continuity is a real but slower-moving problem than a single point of failure for a time-sensitive external contact.

### R10 — a pilot vendor's terms prohibit collection (Dell, already materialised)

**What materialising looks like:** this one already happened, on day one of source evaluation, and is included specifically because it is the clearest example in the whole register of a risk the project chose to face honestly rather than route around quietly. Dell's `robots.txt` disallows collection of the one source that was, on every technical merit, the best-shaped catalogue among the four pilots. The signal that this risk is present in the *general* case (not just this one instance) would be a newly registered source, going forward, that turns out to have a similarly disqualifying compliance status discovered only after significant collector-building effort has already gone into it — a sign the registration-time check (which is supposed to happen *before* any collector work, per DATA_SOURCES.md) was skipped or rushed.

**First action to take, generalised beyond Dell specifically:** verify `robots_policy_status` mechanically (a direct fetch and read of `robots.txt`, not an assumption) at the moment a source is proposed, before any collector-config authoring begins — this ordering is already the stated policy, and the concrete action when a new instance of this risk appears is simply to hold that ordering under time pressure: it is tempting to build the collector first ("I'll check compliance before I turn it on") specifically because the technical work is the fun part, and Dell is the standing example of why that temptation is exactly backwards. When it does occur, follow the same path Dell took: register the source, mark it disabled with the specific disqualifying status recorded, document it publicly (as DATA_SOURCES.md does), and look for either an official API alternative or explicit vendor permission rather than treating the disqualification as unresolvable.
