# Licensing

> Expands [blueprint §3.7](blueprint.md#37-agpl-protects-the-business--challenged-apache-20), [§3.8](blueprint.md#38-share-alike-for-the-dataset-odbl--challenged-cc-by-40--contractual-terms), and the licensing row of [§6](blueprint.md#6-architecture-decision-summary). Decisions here are recorded in [ADR-0009](../adr/0009-code-and-data-licensing.md); this document is the fuller explanation and the practical, contributor-facing summary. **This document is not legal advice, and §8 lists every question in it that still requires qualified legal review before FirmScout relies on the answer commercially.**

## 1. Four separate licensing decisions

FirmScout deliberately treats licensing as four distinct decisions, not one. Conflating them — licensing "the project" under a single term — either over-restricts the code or under-protects the data, because code and a factual database are different kinds of work under different areas of law (copyright licensing for code; database right, contract, and jurisdiction-dependent doctrines for data), and a hosted service's terms of use are a third thing entirely, separate again from the terms governing programmatic API access.

| Decision | Governs | Chosen instrument |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Source code licence** | The Go/TypeScript/YAML-schema source in this repository | Apache-2.0 |
| **Dataset licence** | The catalogue of facts FirmScout publishes | CC BY 4.0 (delayed public snapshots) + contractual FirmScout Data Terms (live feed, enriched fields) |
| **Hosted service terms** | Use of the public website (firmscout.dev) | Standard hosted terms of service (drafted separately; not a licence) |
| **API terms** | Programmatic access via an API key, at any tier | Standard API terms (drafted separately; incorporates the dataset licence terms above by reference for the data returned) |

Each is discussed in turn below. The rest of this document keeps these four cleanly separated — a statement about the code licence never implies anything about the dataset licence, and vice versa.

## 2. Source code: Apache-2.0

**The reasoning, from blueprint §3.7:** the brief's original proposal was AGPL-3.0, on the theory that network copyleft protects FirmScout's business by forcing a competitor who runs the code as a service to contribute changes back. That threat model does not match FirmScout's actual moat. **The code is not what a competitor lacks.** A competitor with the complete source still has none of: the curated source registry (which vendors, which URLs, which are official), the operational history that tunes scheduling intervals and confidence thresholds, the accumulated fixture corpus, or the reliability record that lets an enterprise buyer trust the data enough to pay for it. Those are the actual moat, and AGPL protects none of them.

Meanwhile AGPL has a real, measurable cost: **corporate legal departments routinely block AGPL as a dependency**, full stop, regardless of the specific use case, because AGPL's network-copyleft obligations are broad and its boundaries are genuinely unclear in practice (does "modified" include a private patch that only changes deployment configuration? case law is thin). FirmScout's own assumption P5 — that community contributors, specifically infrastructure engineers who often work for exactly the kind of enterprise that blocks AGPL, will maintain collectors for vendors they personally operate — depends on those contributors' employers being comfortable with the licence of what they are contributing to. AGPL directly works against P5. Apache-2.0 does not have this problem: it is one of the most enterprise-legal-friendly permissive licences available.

**The patent grant.** Apache-2.0 §3 includes an express patent licence from every contributor to every user, terminating automatically for anyone who initiates patent litigation against the project over the licensed work. This is a meaningful protection Apache-2.0 has that simpler permissive licences (MIT, BSD) do not: a contributor's employer cannot contribute code and later assert a patent covering it against FirmScout or its users. Given that infrastructure and security tooling is exactly the kind of domain where patent risk is non-trivial, this is one of the concrete reasons Apache-2.0 was chosen over MIT, not just AGPL.

**Corporate contributor friendliness.** Beyond the patent grant, Apache-2.0's explicit contribution-licensing terms (§5) and its status as a known quantity to essentially every corporate open-source policy make it the licence least likely to require a contributor to seek internal legal sign-off before submitting a pull request — directly serving P5.

**Why AGPL was rejected, restated plainly:** AGPL would have asked FirmScout to pay a real, ongoing cost (suppressed corporate contribution) for a protection (network-copyleft on the code) that does not defend the thing actually worth defending (the data and the operational history). The protection that matters lives in the dataset licence and the hosted/API terms instead — see §3 and §4.

## 3. Dataset: CC BY 4.0 for delayed public snapshots, plus contractual FirmScout Data Terms for the live feed

**The two-tier structure, from blueprint §3.8:**

- **CC BY 4.0** (Creative Commons Attribution 4.0 International) applies to **delayed public snapshots** — periodic exports of the catalogue, generated from PostgreSQL per blueprint §11, released after a deliberate delay from the moment a fact is first published live. The delay period itself is a product parameter, not fixed by this document or by ADR-0009.
- **Separate, contractual FirmScout Data Terms** govern the **live API feed** and any **enriched fields** (confidence scoring detail, structured advisory affected-ranges, source health metadata, and similar Professional/Enterprise-tier content per the capability matrix in blueprint §5) — these are not covered by CC BY 4.0 at all, and access to them is governed by whatever terms a consumer agreed to when obtaining API access (§4).

**Why ODbL's share-alike was rejected as a deal-breaker for enterprise buyers:** ODbL (the Open Database License) is the natural first reflex for a factual database — it is explicitly designed for databases, unlike Creative Commons licences pre-4.0. Its problem for FirmScout specifically is its share-alike clause, which is viral into "derived databases." **A customer's internal asset-management database becomes a derived database the moment they import FirmScout data into it** — that is not an edge case, it is the primary paid use case (blueprint §7 "Distribution" context, and the whole point of the Professional/Enterprise tiers in blueprint §5: automation and integration into a customer's own pipeline). Requiring that customer's internal, proprietary asset database to inherit ODbL's share-alike obligations is precisely the kind of term an enterprise legal department vetoes on sight, and it is also, honestly, close to unenforceable against ordinary internal, non-redistributed use — so it would function as a customer-relationship irritant far more than as real protection. ODbL was rejected on both grounds: it blocks the sale FirmScout needs to make, and it does not reliably deliver the protection it promises in exchange.

**Why CC BY 4.0 was chosen, specifically because it explicitly covers the EU *sui generis* database right:** the earlier Open Data Commons licences (ODC-BY, ODbL, PDDL) were written *before* Creative Commons 4.0 existed, at a time when CC licences were understood to cover copyright but were ambiguous about database rights, which is exactly why the ODC family exists at all. **Creative Commons 4.0 licences (including CC BY 4.0) were rewritten to explicitly extend to "Sui Generis Database Rights"** as CC 4.0's own legal code defines that term — meaning CC BY 4.0, unlike its 3.0 predecessor and unlike a plain copyright licence, is drafted to license the specific EU database right that protects a compilation like FirmScout's catalogue even where the individual facts within it are not copyrightable. This closes the gap ODbL exists to fill, without ODbL's share-alike cost, for the delayed-snapshot tier. **Attribution-only** (no share-alike, no non-commercial restriction) is deliberate: it lets anyone — including a competitor, including a commercial reuser — build on the delayed public snapshot freely, which is consistent with the "genuinely useful public site, not a crippled one" principle in blueprint §5, while the live feed and enriched fields remain the commercial product.

## 4. The factual-data question, stated honestly

This section exists because glossing over it would be dishonest, and because it is precisely the kind of question a founding team is not positioned to resolve on its own:

- **Raw facts may not be copyrightable in some jurisdictions.** In the United States specifically, *Feist Publications v. Rural Telephone Service* (1991) rejected "sweat of the brow" as a basis for copyright and held that a mere factual compilation, arranged in an unoriginal (e.g. purely alphabetical or purely chronological) way, is not copyrightable. "RouterOS 7.24.2 was released on 2026-09-02" is a fact; a bare list of such facts sorted by date is exactly the kind of compilation *Feist* says copyright does not reach. FirmScout's dataset licence choices assume there is *something* to license — a database right, or protection in the selection/arrangement, or a contractual basis independent of any underlying IP right — that may not exist, or may not exist in the form assumed, in every jurisdiction.
- **Database rights differ materially between the EU, UK, and US.** The EU's *sui generis* database right (Database Directive 96/9/EC) protects a database reflecting "substantial investment" in obtaining, verifying, or presenting its contents — independent of, and broader than, ordinary copyright, and it is the right CC BY 4.0 is drafted to cover (§3). The UK retained a version of this right post-Brexit, but UK and EU database rights have begun to diverge in scope and in whose investment qualifies (an EU-only database right may no longer protect a UK-based maker's database in the EU, and vice versa, depending on the specifics — an area of live legal uncertainty, not a settled question this document can answer). The US has **no equivalent *sui generis* database right at all** — protection there, if any, comes from ordinary copyright in the selection/arrangement (and even that is constrained by *Feist*), or from contract law (the FirmScout Data Terms), which only binds parties who actually agreed to them.
- **Selection and arrangement may be protectable where the facts are not.** *Feist* itself acknowledges that a compilation can be copyrightable in its *selection, coordination, or arrangement* of facts, even when no individual fact is protectable, provided that selection/arrangement involves at least a modicum of creativity. FirmScout's curated source registry — which vendors are tracked, which sources are deemed official, how products are categorised and cross-referenced by alias (blueprint §8.1's Catalog context) — is a stronger candidate for this kind of protection than the raw release-date facts themselves are. This is a meaningful distinction the eventual legal review should examine specifically, rather than treating "the dataset" as one undifferentiated thing for licensing purposes.

**The honest summary:** FirmScout licenses its dataset as though it has clear rights to license, because doing so gives downstream users clear terms to rely on and gives FirmScout a contractual basis for its commercial tier regardless of how the underlying IP question resolves in any given jurisdiction — but the underlying IP question is genuinely unresolved, jurisdiction-dependent, and listed explicitly in §8 as requiring qualified legal review before this framing is relied upon in a dispute.

## 5. Attribution requirements

A downstream user of a CC BY 4.0 delayed snapshot must, per the licence's standard terms: (1) give appropriate credit to FirmScout, (2) provide a link to the licence, and (3) indicate if changes were made to the data — all in any reasonable manner, but not in a way that suggests FirmScout endorses the downstream use. In practice, for a typical reuse (importing the snapshot into an internal tool, republishing a derived report), this means retaining a visible credit such as "Firmware version data from FirmScout (firmscout.dev), CC BY 4.0" near the reused data or in the reusing product's documentation/about page, and not presenting the data as if it originated with the downstream user. This requirement applies specifically to the CC BY 4.0-licensed delayed snapshot; use of the live feed or enriched fields under the FirmScout Data Terms is subject to whatever attribution terms those contractual terms specify, which may differ (and, being contractual rather than a public licence, are not summarised further in this document — see the terms themselves once drafted).

## 6. Third-party content

- **Release notes are linked, not reproduced in full.** FirmScout's product pages and API responses point to the vendor's own release-notes URL (`source.url` in every `Release`/`Product` response, api.md §3) rather than mirroring the vendor's copyrighted text. This is both a licensing discipline and a maintenance one — a linked source stays current with the vendor's own edits; a mirrored copy silently goes stale and additionally reproduces content FirmScout has no licence to redistribute (blueprint §4.2, "not a full-text mirror of release notes").
- **Evidence excerpts are kept concise and used for verification, never as a substitute for the source.** Every candidate and published release carries an `Evidence.Excerpt` (collector-sdk.md §3.6) — the minimal text that supports the extracted fact, bounded in length by the extraction engine regardless of what a collector config's `evidence_excerpt` selector matches (collector-config-spec.md §3.5). Its purpose is letting a reviewer or a consumer verify that a fact was actually stated by the source, not letting FirmScout serve as an alternative place to read the vendor's content. This is the same "concise, for verification" line the fixture-recording guidance in collector-sdk.md §9 draws for test fixtures — the two rules are the same principle applied in two places.
- **Vendor trademark usage for identification only.** "MikroTik", "RouterOS", "Fortinet", and every other vendor or product name appearing on FirmScout is used purely to identify the vendor's own product being described — nominative fair use, not an assertion of affiliation, sponsorship, or endorsement by that vendor. FirmScout does not use a vendor's logo or brand styling beyond what is needed for identification (e.g. a small, clearly-labelled vendor icon on a listing), and nothing on the site or in API responses should read as though a vendor produced or endorses FirmScout's content about them.

## 7. Contributor licensing: DCO sign-off, not a CLA

FirmScout uses the **Developer Certificate of Origin (DCO)** — a `Signed-off-by` line on every commit, certifying the contributor has the right to submit the contribution under the project's licence — rather than a Contributor License Agreement (CLA) requiring a separate, out-of-band agreement (often with a legal entity, sometimes requiring counter-signature) before a contribution can be merged.

**The reasoning and the trade-off, stated plainly:**

- **A CLA would enable relicensing later.** A CLA typically grants the project (or a company behind it) broader rights than the project's own outbound licence — commonly including the right to relicense the contributed code, including under a different or even proprietary licence, without needing to track down and re-obtain permission from every past contributor individually. This is valuable optionality for a project that might, for example, need to dual-license code commercially later, or that anticipates changing its outbound licence.
- **DCO lowers the contribution barrier.** A DCO sign-off is a single flag on a `git commit`, requires no account creation on a third-party CLA-signing service, no legal review by a first-time contributor's employer (in most cases — a company with strict IP-assignment policies may still want to review even a DCO commitment, but the friction is far lower than a CLA's), and no separate agreement to track outside of git history itself, which is itself the DCO's audit trail.
- **The project chooses DCO.** Given assumption P5 — community contributors, specifically ones affiliated with the vendors and enterprises FirmScout most needs collectors from, are the population most likely to be deterred by CLA friction — and given that Apache-2.0 (§2) was already chosen specifically to minimise corporate friction, a CLA would work against the same goal the code licence choice serves. The trade-off accepted is real: DCO does **not** grant relicensing flexibility the way a CLA would, so a future decision to relicense the codebase (to a different open-source licence, or dual-licensed commercially) would require either unanimous past-contributor consent or accepting that only newly-contributed code carries the new terms. That constraint is accepted as the cost of the lower contribution barrier DCO provides.

## 8. Trademark policy for the FirmScout name

The **"FirmScout" name and any associated logo are not covered by the Apache-2.0 licence**, consistent with standard open-source practice (Apache-2.0 §6 explicitly excludes trademark rights from the grant) — permission to use, modify, and redistribute the *code* under Apache-2.0 does not include permission to call a modified or redistributed version "FirmScout," or to imply it is the official FirmScout project or service. A full, published trademark policy (naming conventions for forks, acceptable use in blog posts and integrations, when a self-hosted deployment may or may not call itself "FirmScout") is one of the items requiring legal input before it is finalised (§9) — the interim, working principle is: **self-hosting the unmodified or lightly-configured project for internal or community use is fine and encouraged; presenting a fork, a competing hosted service, or a modified dataset as "FirmScout" or as officially affiliated with FirmScout is not**, and community members are asked to use a clearly distinct name for anything in the second category pending the formal policy.

## 9. Questions requiring qualified legal review

**This is the load-bearing section of this document.** Everything above states the founding team's reasoning and current position; none of it is a substitute for review by a qualified lawyer, and several of the positions above are explicitly provisional pending that review. Each question below is phrased so a lawyer can answer it directly, not as a vague area of concern:

1. **Dataset licence choice.** Is CC BY 4.0 the correct instrument to license FirmScout's delayed public snapshot, given that CC 4.0's coverage of the EU *sui generis* database right is well-established but its interaction with US factual-compilation law (post-*Feist*) and UK post-Brexit database right is less so — or does the dataset need a supplementary licence, a different licence entirely, or jurisdiction-specific licensing terms?
2. **Database rights by jurisdiction.** What rights, if any, actually attach to FirmScout's catalogue as compiled, separately in the EU, the UK, and the US, and does the answer differ for the raw fact rows versus the curated selection/arrangement (source registry, categorisation, aliasing) described in §4?
3. **Permissibility of collecting from sources whose `robots.txt` disallows it.** Beyond FirmScout's own policy of simply not collecting from such sources (DATA_SOURCES.md, blueprint §3.6) — is there any legal exposure (e.g. under the US Computer Fraud and Abuse Act, EU equivalents, or a source's terms of service) from *registering* such a source as disabled/documented in a public repository, or from later collecting it after obtaining explicit vendor permission that is itself informal (an email, rather than a signed agreement)?
4. **Use of undocumented vendor update APIs.** Ubiquiti's `fw-update.ubnt.com/api/firmware-latest` (the substitute pilot for the "clean API" profile per blueprint §3.6) has no published API terms of use and no `robots.txt` restriction — what is the legal basis, if any, for treating "no restriction stated" as permission, and does automated, low-volume, polite use of an undocumented but publicly reachable JSON endpoint create any exposure distinct from documented API use?
5. **Evidence excerpt length and fair dealing/fair use.** Is the current, informal "concise, bounded, for verification" standard for `Evidence.Excerpt` (§6) actually within fair use (US) or fair dealing (UK/other jurisdictions with a fair dealing doctrine) for the range of content types involved (HTML changelog snippets, PDF release-note excerpts, RSS advisory summaries) — and should the engine's excerpt length bound (collector-config-spec.md §3.5) be set to a specific, legally-informed maximum rather than the current informal "kept concise" guidance?
6. **Data removal requests from vendors.** DATA_SOURCES.md's removal-request process withdraws affected facts (marks them `withdrawn` with reason and evidence retained) rather than deleting them outright, preserving the audit trail — is withdrawal-with-retained-audit-trail a legally sufficient response to every plausible form of vendor removal request, or are there circumstances (e.g. a request citing a specific legal basis rather than a general preference) where actual deletion, not withdrawal, would be legally required?
7. **Hosted service terms.** Standard terms of service for the public website need drafting from scratch — acceptable use, disclaimers regarding data accuracy (particularly given blueprint §4.2's explicit statement that FirmScout is not patch-management or vulnerability-scanning advice), liability limitation, and how those terms interact with the dataset licence terms in §3.
8. **API terms.** Terms governing programmatic access at every tier, including how they incorporate or reference the FirmScout Data Terms (§3) for data returned by the API, key-holder obligations, and termination/suspension conditions — currently entirely undrafted.
9. **DCO versus CLA.** Is DCO sign-off (§7) sufficient for the project's likely future needs, including the specific relicensing/dual-licensing scenarios the founding team can currently foresee (e.g. an enterprise on-prem offering with different terms) — or does a specific foreseeable scenario make a CLA's relicensing flexibility worth its contribution-friction cost after all?
10. **Trademark registration.** Should "FirmScout" be formally registered as a trademark, in which jurisdiction(s), for which goods/services classes, and what does that registration status imply for the scope and enforceability of the interim trademark policy in §8?
